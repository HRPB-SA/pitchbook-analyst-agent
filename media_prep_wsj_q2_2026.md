# WSJ Media Prep: Q2 2026 Global Unicorn Tracker ("The One-Horned Market")

Anticipated questions and suggested answers for press interviews on the Q2 2026
Global Unicorn Tracker (published August 11, 2026). Voice: the Late-Stage
Research team. All figures as of June 30, 2026 unless noted; July prints
(SpaceX $113.50 close, Cerebras -39.4%) are cited in the report itself.
Every number below is cross-checked against the published PDF.

Key messages to land in any interview:

1. Concentration is the finding. One name or a handful carries every total:
   deals, exits, secondaries, index return.
2. The calm in the middle is silence, not health. Six down rounds reflect a
   universe that has stopped generating prices, and the dormant path is the
   structural story through 2027.
3. The Q2 index gain was a claim about where SpaceX would trade, not about
   private company fundamentals, and that claim is now tested in public daily.

Bridging note for post-June-30 events: the August 6 SpaceX lockup has now
passed. Inputs are capped at June 30 (plus cited July prints), so
characterize the framework (clean absorption keeps an October Anthropic
listing live; a further leg down pushes the queue to 2027) and defer
specifics to the Q3 tracker.

## 1. Framing

**Q: Your own index just posted its best quarter in over a decade, and your
report spends 20 pages telling readers not to trust it. Which is it?**

A: Both, and that is the point of the title. The 42.7% quarter is real
arithmetic and misleading economics. Three trading days produced 26.4 of the
42.7 percentage points, and all three were repricings of companies that had
gone public: SpaceX and Cerebras. Strip those days and the private market
returned 16.3% against the public benchmark's 14.9%, essentially parity. The
gain was a claim about where SpaceX would trade, and that claim is now tested
in public every day. It is already reversing: marked at late-July prices,
SpaceX and Cerebras carry roughly $687 billion less than the index assigned
them on June 30.

**Q: Why "The One-Horned Market"?**

A: Because wherever you cut the data you find a spike, not a spread. The top
five rounds were 60.1% of Q2 financing, and Anthropic alone was 44.9%. SpaceX
was 94.9% of Q2 exit value; the next five exits combined were 4%. Five names
account for a third of all secondary volume since 2024. AI is 61.7% of
unicorn value. For any LP not on the SpaceX cap table, the record quarter did
not happen. The market has one horn.

**Q: Is this a bubble? Is it 2021 again?**

A: It is structurally the opposite of 2021. That year was broad: 1,894
rounds and an investor base three times today's size. This year is narrow:
$396.1 billion through just 444 rounds, 995 distinct investors, and 71% of
every global venture dollar flowing to 2% of the deal count. The 2021 risk
was that everything was overpriced. The 2026 risk is that almost nothing is
priced: 47.6% of unicorns have not set a price in over two years. Whether the
dozen names being repriced weekly are overpriced is exactly what the
Anthropic and OpenAI listings will test.

## 2. The dormant path

**Q: Nearly half the unicorns in your universe haven't raised in over two
years. Are they still worth a billion dollars? Are these zombie companies?**

A: The honest answer is nobody knows, and that is the finding. 828 of 1,743
active unicorns, 47.6%, have not set a price in more than two years, and
37.1% have gone more than three. On their last-round marks they carry 22.2%
of the $8.2 trillion aggregate, roughly $1.8 trillion, which is about the
same paper value Anthropic and OpenAI carry between just the two of them. We
would not call them all zombies: the cohort includes real businesses that
simply do not need capital (Ant Group last raised in August 2020, SHEIN in
January 2024). But a 2020 or 2021 mark tells you what one syndicate paid
years ago, not what the company is worth now.

**Q: Down rounds fell from 31 last year to six. Isn't that good news?**

A: It is the most misread number in this market. A failure is recorded only
when a company raises at a lower price, and the 828 dormant companies, by
definition, cannot print one. So six down rounds reflects the universe's
silence as much as its health. The companies that did raise genuinely got
strong terms: a 2.2x median step-up and one year between rounds. But an
investor pricing a round today has no observable cohort of failed raises to
calibrate against, because the companies that would supply that signal are
not in the market.

**Q: So what actually happens to these 828 companies? What is the path out?**

A: There are four doors, and we can measure how open each one is. Door one is
a new primary round: open for AI and for quality, mostly shut otherwise; only
614 of 1,743 companies have raised in the past 12 months, and none of the H1
megacapital reached the dormant tier. Door two is a secondary price:
effectively shut, because the 20 largest names absorb $7 of every $10 of
secondary volume and depth at the periphery is thinning. Door three is an
exit: the IPO route is open mainly for the very largest, and the record
punishes them (4 of 31 listings above $10 billion beat the benchmark over 180
days). The realistic exit is strategic M&A, which quietly reopened: $50.5
billion in H1 excluding xAI versus $18.9 billion in all of 2025, with the
average deal roughly quadrupling in size. A $3 billion strategic sale is
available in a way it was not in 2023. Door four is none of the above: stay
silent, spend down, and drift below the threshold without ever printing a
price, which our failure statistics cannot see. The dormant cohort needs a
funding market that broadens or an exit market that extends below the
megadeal tier. Neither is visible in the H1 data, and M&A is the one to
watch, because it is the only door whose hinges moved this half.

**Q: How many of them are actually dead? What is the real failure rate?**

A: We can tell you what we can count: 245 unicorns have fallen below the
billion-dollar line since 2016, and failures are severe when they occur, with
a median failing valuation of $524.5 million and a median 67% decline from
peak. We can also tell you the count understates losses: the 15 companies
from the 2021 cohort that fell at exit are recorded as exits, not failures,
and a failure requires a price that dormant companies have stopped
generating. What we will not do is invent a body count. The recorded number
is a floor, not an estimate, and we believe the untested majority is where
the real risk lies.

**Q: If nobody is pricing them, why should anyone believe your $8.2 trillion
headline, or the $1.7 billion median?**

A: They should not treat either as a mark to market, and the report says so.
The aggregate rose from $7.3 trillion to $8.2 trillion on roughly a dozen
upward repricings, with Anthropic, OpenAI, and ByteDance supplying the bulk.
The median unicorn is worth $1.7 billion measured by its last round, and that
median is stale by construction. An institution benchmarking to the aggregate
is tracking a dozen repricings and crediting them to 1,743 companies, most of
which no one has priced. We publish the number because it is the industry's
reference point, and we spend the report showing why to handle it with
gloves.

**Q: Does the dormant cohort pose a systemic risk? Who is holding the bag?**

A: It reaches LPs through stale fund NAVs, and two mechanics matter. First,
the investor base has shrunk by two-thirds while syndicates have grown, with
six-plus-investor rounds now 60.9% of deals, so the same names recur across
cap tables: a repricing in one large position reaches several managers at
once. That is correlation risk dressed as diversification. Second, because a
failure requires a price, NAVs on the dormant tier can sit unchanged for
years and then adjust all at once when a company finally transacts. It is not
a bank-run mechanism; it is a slow information problem that resolves in
lumps.

**Q: Name names. Which dormant unicorns are in trouble?**

A: We do not name companies we cannot price; that discipline is the point of
the report. What we can give you is the observable perimeter. We publish the
age distribution of marks, and where a price does exist we cite it: Ripple
trades at half its last round with a pending listing, Kraken 41% below. Below
the top 20 there is no price to cite, and inventing one on the record is how
stale marks got here in the first place.

## 3. Concentration, AI, and quality

**Q: Anthropic raised $65 billion at $965 billion, and you score it 6.8,
eleventh of 18. Are you saying the two most valuable private companies are
overvalued?**

A: The rank is about the business, not the price, and we are explicit that
the private market prices scarcity and scale, not our score: nine of the 18
trade above their last round and nine below, with no relationship to quality.
What the score explains is why the model builders sit at the bottom of the
framework: compute independence. Companies that build frontier models average
3.7 on it against 7.5 for the platforms' strategic velocity, because they
rent the input that determines whether they can keep operating, and in
OpenAI's case control still rests with the nonprofit foundation. Whether $965
billion is right gets answered in the order book, and consensus has that test
in October.

**Q: Your median AI company scores 4.6 and 140 of 301 are in the distressed
tier. Is the AI middle a bubble waiting to burst?**

A: What we would say is that value has run far ahead of quality in the
middle: only five of 301 US AI companies reach the strong tier. The scores do
not predict a crash. What they predict is price discovery: the weaker a
business scores, the fewer independent prices exist to test its valuation. So
if repricing comes for the AI middle, it will come late and all at once.
Quality is not the loudest part of the AI story, but it is what the
valuations ultimately rest on, and for most of these companies it is lacking.

**Q: Prometheus raised $18 billion within seven months of launch, with
JPMorgan, Goldman, and BlackRock on the cap table. What does that tell you?**

A: That the marginal dollar at the top is no longer a venture fund; it is an
institutional balance sheet. That changes price sensitivity, and it changes
the exit these companies need, because only a very large public listing fits
that ownership. It is the clearest sign the megadeal tier has become a
different asset class from the rest of venture. And for the 828 dormant
companies, none of this capital is available.

**Q: Anthropic shipped a design tool and Figma lost 90% against the index.
Are the AI labs eating the last generation of unicorns?**

A: Figma is the cautionary tale: a 250% first-day pop unwound to a 90.9%
shortfall within 180 days, with the sharpest drop after Anthropic's April
launch of a competing design product. The pattern to watch is that AI value
accrual shows up as markups inside the private index and as markdowns in the
public comps, including companies that were themselves unicorns 18 months
ago. The same force inflating the private aggregate is deflating parts of the
public one.

**Q: What should an allocator actually do with this report?**

A: Three things. First, recognize that a late-stage venture allocation is now
a concentrated AI bet whether or not the mandate says so: AI is 61.7% of
value, and only five of 11 verticals beat public equities over the trailing
year across a 113-point spread. Second, count your overlap: with 995
investors and fuller syndicates, one repricing reaches several of your
managers at once. Third, ask managers what fraction of NAV was priced within
24 months. That number, not the $8.2 trillion headline, is the health of the
book.

## 4. The IPO window and the forecast

**Q: You are predicting more IPOs in the second half. How confident are you?**

A: The mechanism is underpricing leading issuance, and it is old: Ibbotson
and Jaffe documented it in 1975, Lowry and Schwert settled the direction in
2002, and our data reproduces it. Across the 35 quarters since 2016 with at
least three unicorn listings, a quarter's median first-day gain carries a
+0.42 rank correlation with the next quarter's listing count, while the
backward pairing collapses to +0.03. That asymmetry is the test of direction.
We are honest about magnitude: exclude 2020 and 2021 and the correlation
drops to +0.29. So direction yes, size modest. Q2's 16.3% median first-day
gain says come; the record on the largest deals says be careful once you
arrive.

**Q: Will Anthropic's October IPO happen? What could derail it?**

A: The gating item was August 6, when SpaceX's lockup released roughly 900
million insider shares into a stock already below its $135 issue price. The
two largest names in the queue are timing against that price, not their own
readiness. Our published line: a clean absorption keeps October live, and a
further leg down pushes both listings into 2027 regardless of what the
companies' books look like. There is also a contradiction they are pricing
into: the issuance signal says now, while the record says the largest deals
perform worst, with the median $10 billion-plus unicorn listing trailing the
benchmark by 43.8% at 180 days.

**Q: SpaceX made the quarter and now trades below issue. Was the IPO a
failure? Did the buyers get burned?**

A: Two halves. The listing priced roughly 30% above the final private mark,
so private holders' marks were conservative, not inflated; the same was true
of Cerebras. The aftermarket is the other half: Q2's listings trailed the
global index by a median 6.8% within 30 days, and the hotter the open, the
more the aftermarket reclaimed, a -0.49 rank correlation in the quarter. For
a buyer at the open, the base rate across 290 unicorn IPOs since 2016 is that
the share beating the index falls from 42.8% at day seven to 27.3% at day
180. That is not a SpaceX statement; it is the record.

**Q: A first-day pop signal in one breath, "pops mean nothing" in the next.
Which is it?**

A: They answer different questions. Within a cohort, a pop tells you nothing
about how that stock performs later: across 283 listings the correlation
between first-day gain and subsequent excess return is +0.009. Across time,
a quarter of hot pricing tells you something about how many companies list
next: +0.42. The pop is a poor stock signal and a decent calendar signal.

## 5. Index integrity

**Q: Your record private-market quarter was produced by public companies. Is
the index broken?**

A: The index did what it is designed to do: carry constituents at observable
prices, and the newest observable prices were public prints. What we push
back on is the reading of broad private appreciation. The 120-day correlation
with public equities fell to +0.46 from an average of +0.88, yet private
index volatility ran about twice the benchmark's; an index that had genuinely
decoupled would not swing more than public markets do. We are forecasting
that the Q3 print gives back a substantial share of the Q2 gain without any
deterioration in the underlying companies, because the gain was never a claim
about them.

## 6. Secondary market

**Q: Will the secondary market still exist in 2027?**

A: That is the live question, and we posed it deliberately. The market is
real, $8.9 billion since 2024, and it is narrowing: the top 20 names take $7
of every $10, and the five largest are a third of lifetime value. SpaceX is
public, Cerebras is public, Cursor is under an acquisition agreement, and
Anthropic and OpenAI have filed. Whether the next tier trades enough to fill
that gap decides whether this market exists in 2027, and nothing in the Q2
data says it will. If it does not, the dormant problem worsens, because
secondaries were the one place a company could get a price without raising.

**Q: Ramp raised at $44 billion and a month later the secondary market had it
13.8% lower. Which price is right?**

A: Both are facts about different buyers. A primary round shows what one
syndicate would pay for a negotiated, structured position; the tape shows the
marginal buyer. That is why we treat secondaries as a third reference point.
When the test came, the tape was conservative at the top: Cerebras and SpaceX
both listed roughly 30% above their final private marks. And where the tape
disagrees loudly, Ripple at half its last round and Kraken 41% below, both
with pending listings, a buyer at the primary mark is underwriting a rerating
the secondary market has already declined to make.

## 7. Methodology challenges (be ready)

**Q: SaaS and AI hold $12.5 trillion but your whole universe is $8.2
trillion. How can that be?**

A: Two reasons, both footnoted on the chart. Verticals are not mutually
exclusive, so a company tagged SaaS and AI counts in both, and the vertical
cut includes exited unicorns, while the $8.2 trillion aggregate is
active-only and deduplicated. It is a taxonomy overlay, not a partition;
that is also why space tech shows $1.9 trillion across only 34 companies.

**Q: Your quality scores are not comparable to last quarter's. Convenient.
What changed?**

A: We rebaselined the methodology and said so in the report rather than
quietly restating. The dimensions and weights are published, inputs are
capped at June 30 with post-quarter events excluded, and we carry wide
confidence bands because private disclosure is thin. We would rather flag a
break in comparability than let readers draw a false trend.

**Q: PitchBook scores the constituents of an index it publishes with
Morningstar. Conflict of interest?**

A: The scores play no role in index construction, which tracks the largest
and most liquid unicorns under a published Morningstar methodology. And note
what this report does: it argues against taking our own index's headline
quarter at face value, names the three days that produced it, and forecasts
the Q3 give-back. That is not what a marketing document does.

## 8. The closer

**Q: What is the one number to watch next quarter?**

A: Fresh-price coverage: the share of the universe priced within 24 months,
52.4% today. If it rises, the dormant path is resolving through raises and
exits, and the mid-market M&A count (16 strategic deals in H1) is the door to
watch. If it falls while the headline aggregate climbs, the one-horned
problem is getting worse: more value resting on fewer prices. And for the
index, watch whether the Q3 print gives back the Q2 gain as we forecast;
SpaceX and Cerebras were already roughly $687 billion below their June 30
carrying values by late July.
