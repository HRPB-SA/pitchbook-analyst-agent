Durability, IPOs, and the Duopoly Myth

Three questions the AI market keeps asking, answered with the data underneath them: what actually makes an AI startup defensible, why the biggest companies are staying private, and whether "two winners per category" is a real pattern or a story we tell because funding rounds are easy to count.

SEG 01
What does the data show on durability?

Defensibility is measurable, and the tool already has a memory

The panel's opening question, "does the exit predictor have historical data or only a real-time score?", has a clean answer: it keeps history. Every VC-backed company PitchBook scores carries a Historical Predictions chart: success, IPO, and M&A probability lines tracked over time, with financing rounds overlaid and analyst notes explaining the swings. So the compelling angle is on the table. You can ask, for a specific company or a screened cohort, whether a defensibility score has climbed or decayed as the model got cheaper to copy. The one caveat worth stating plainly: the productized time series lives at the company level; a vertical-wide "have vertical-AI scores improved?" trend is a cohort pull, not a built-in chart.

The IA40's churn is itself the durability signal. Across five annual lists, only Databricks has survived every cut. Application-layer names rotate out; the infrastructure layer compounds. Madrona x PitchBook IA40, 2021 to 2025

PitchBook's own research lands in the same place from the data side. Its Q1 2026 note "SaaS Is Dead, Long Live SaS" calls the SaaS-pocalypse a "false prophecy" and reframes the winners as Service-as-Software: pricing shifts from roughly $1,200 per seat per year to about $10,000 per automated workflow, an ~8x unit uplift as the software TAM merges with the labor TAM. The recurring guidance across notes is blunt: avoid funding UI wrappers, back vertical agents with proprietary data moats, and treat 60% gross margin as the AI-era durability line. Early-stage SaaS still posts the single highest expected successful-exit rate of any vertical, 72.1%, top-ranked for eight years running, while AI early-stage deal value grew 41.2% over the trailing year against a 17.5% cross-vertical average. Durability is not dead; it moved up the stack from the model to the workflow it is embedded in.

State of AI IPOs

The drought is a mirage. The value is real, it is just private

Databricks: raising a mega-round at $134B, on purpose

The reason a company this large keeps raising privately is not weakness, it is arithmetic. Private capital has been cheap and abundant, and tender and secondary markets provide employee liquidity without the exposure of a public listing. CEO Ali Ghodsi has called 2026 "a terrible year to go public." The valuation staircase below is the record of a company that never needed the exit.

Anthropic: timing into a moving target

Anthropic re-rated from $380B to $965B in three months and filed confidentially in June 2026 for an expected October listing. The risk is not demand, it is the revenue narrative: an enterprise shift away from "token maxing" toward efficiency, plus export-control uncertainty on the Alphabet-chip supply it just raised $34.5B of debt to buy. Revenue is still accelerating; the question is whether it prints one more clean quarter first.

Read the fine print on 2026's IPOs Cerebras listed in May 2026 at $40.6B and reported ~$248M of TTM net income, which reads as "profitable AI chip IPO". The asterisk: that GAAP profit is largely a ~$363M non-cash warrant gain tied to its OpenAI deal; on a non-GAAP operating basis it lost money, and the stock trades below its first-day close, as do the other 2026 listings (EquipmentShare, BitGo). The exit window is open, but it is pricing for scrutiny, not euphoria.
SEG 04
The idea: two winners in every category

It is not a duopoly. It is winner-take-most, miscounted

The tempting pattern is two names per AI vertical: Harvey and Legora, Anthropic and OpenAI, Exa and Parallel. The data says something sharper and more useful. Genuine two-way splits are the exception. The rule is one company many times larger than whatever sits in second, and often the real business leader never appears in funding data at all.

When you see a "2" in AI, check what it really is: a genuine dead heat, a US champion beside a non-US sovereign-tech champion, a hedge two crossover funds paid for, or a distant runner-up that only looks like a rival because it raised a round.

Geographic bifurcation, not competition

Some "duopolies" are one champion per regulatory bloc. Defense AI splits cleanly into Anduril (US) and Helsing (EU sovereign-tech). Legal AI has the same shadow structure: Harvey is US-rooted, Legora is Stockholm-rooted and pitches European firms. They are a 1 plus 1 serving different buyers, not a head-to-head 2.

Stagnation, not concentration

AI companion apps are the counter-case: Character.ai has been frozen since its founders left for Google in Aug 2024, Replika since 2017. The real winners became features inside Grok and Meta AI rather than standalone venture outcomes. Sometimes a category converges to zero investable names.

The frontier breaks the frame too At the top of the market, "Anthropic vs OpenAI" reads as a clean 1.1x duopoly ($965B vs $852B). But PitchBook's Frontier Five note counts five, not two, and finds a valuation-quality paradox: on its AI Business Quality score, Databricks tops the group at 8.7 and is the only member with positive free cash flow, while OpenAI, the highest-profile name, scores lowest at 4.8. And xAI is not a standalone third, it is $250B folded inside a ~$2.4T SpaceX. The two-name story does not survive contact with the cap tables.

The through-line

Across all three segments the same correction applies: count the business, not the round. Durability is now measurable and has a history you can chart. The IPO drought is a liquidity choice, not an absence of exit-scale companies. And the "duopoly" is usually a winner-take-most market with a nominal second place that funding data overstates. If a thesis rests on round size, it will name the wrong winners.
