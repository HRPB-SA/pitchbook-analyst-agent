### §11 CFA PREP
Domain: Equity — IPO Valuation Methods (rotation position: Equity)
Concept: Three primary approaches to IPO valuation — (1) Comparable company analysis (price/book, EV/EBITDA, P/E vs. peer group); (2) DCF on projected free cash flows; (3) Precedent transaction multiples.
Vignette: SpaceX prices at \$135/share, \$1.77T implied valuation. 2025 revenue: \$18.67B. The implied EV/Revenue multiple is approximately 95x trailing.
Question A: A CFA analyst using comparable company analysis would benchmark SpaceX against which peer group, and why does standard SaaS comps methodology break down for a company with three fundamentally different business lines?
Answer: The correct approach is sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) — Starlink (satellite broadband, comparable to telecom/internet infrastructure), Falcon (aerospace services, comparable to launch/defense contractors), Starship (R&D optionality, no current comp). A blended EV/Revenue multiple applied to the consolidated entity conflates a profitable subscription business (Starlink) with a loss-making development program (Starship/xAI). The 95x trailing multiple is not the "right" number for any segment individually — it reflects the optionality premium on Starship and xAI, priced as if they're already generating Starlink-level returns. Wrong-answer trap: selecting a single tech peer group (e.g., cloud SaaS at 15-20x) understates the growth premium but applying that premium to the full consolidated revenue double-counts the profitable segment.
Harrison connection: The AIBQ xAI/SpaceX composite (4.49) reflects exactly this conflation risk — the CE score is undefined because the revenue base is consolidated and the loss-generating xAI segment has no standalone financials. The same problem a CFA analyst faces in valuation, Harrison faces in AIBQ scoring.

### §14 THE ANALOG
Mechanism: The 1999-2000 dot-com IPO wave saw 547 tech IPOs in 1999 and 406 in 2000. The structural parallel to 2026: a narrow window of institutional appetite for high-multiple, pre-profitability companies, with a fixed set of anchor names (Amazon, eBay then; Anthropic, Databricks now) surrounded by a long tail of lower-quality paper that will be distributed in the same window.
Parallel: Saudi Aramco in 2019 was the last mega-IPO that consumed institutional allocation capacity. It priced at \$1.7T, hit \$2T briefly, then spent two years below IPO price before recovering. The parallel is not the technology — it's the allocation physics.
Where it diverges: The 2026 cohort has real revenue. Anthropic's \$47B ARR and Databricks' FCF-positive status have no dot-com analog. The overvaluation risk is multiple, not business model.
Implication: The relevant risk is not that these companies fail — it's that they succeed at business and still disappoint public market investors because the entry multiple left no room for error. The Morningstar \$780B SpaceX figure is the 2026 equivalent of the Amazon \$400 post-dot-com collapse: right company, wrong price.
