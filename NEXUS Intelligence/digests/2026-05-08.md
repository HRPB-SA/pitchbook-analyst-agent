## 🎓 13. CFA PREP
**Domain:** Ethics — Standard II(B) Market Manipulation
**Concept:** Standard II(B) prohibits practices that distort prices or trading volume with intent to deceive. Two types: information-based (false rumors) and transaction-based (wash trades, pump-and-dump). The most common exam trap is conflating legitimate market making or hedging with manipulation — bid-ask spread maintenance and large-block hedging trades are NOT manipulation even if they affect price, because intent to deceive is absent.
**Practice Question:**
> Marcus Chen, CFA, manages a long-short equity fund with a 3% short position in TechCorp. Two analysts on Chen's team independently reach a negative thesis on TechCorp's accounting practices. Chen invites both analysts to publish their notes simultaneously through industry conferences and financial media. The notes are factually accurate, well-sourced, and Chen discloses his fund's short position in each publication. After publication, TechCorp's stock declines 8% on heavy volume. Has Chen violated Standard II(B) Market Manipulation?
>
> A) Yes, because coordinating simultaneous publication is designed to amplify market impact.
> B) Yes, because Chen profited from the price decline he engineered.
> C) No, because the analysis is accurate and Chen's position is disclosed.
> D) No, because Standard II(B) only applies to transaction-based manipulation, not information dissemination.
**Answer: C.** Three conditions must hold for compliant publication of bearish research while short: (1) factually accurate; (2) firm's position disclosed; (3) research is genuinely the analyst's view, not coordinated with intent to deceive. Chen meets all three.
**Why each wrong answer fails:**

## 📜 19. THE ANALOG
The historical analog to today's signal is **Williams Communications, 2000-2001**. Williams built a 33,000-mile long-haul fiber network in the late 1990s, then in mid-2000 began wholesale-leasing dark-fiber capacity to Tier 2 telecom resellers because its own retail telecom business could not absorb the buildout fast enough. By Q2 2001, Tier 2 demand collapsed as the dot-com bust hit, and the lit-fiber wholesale price dropped 87% in eighteen months. Williams filed for bankruptcy in April 2002. The mechanism: infrastructure built for a primary use case ended up monetized through wholesale to second-tier customers, and when wholesale demand evaporated, the "value" of the network was revealed as overstated — the network was always in excess of primary-use-case demand. The question for today's signal: is xAI/SpaceX's lease of Colossus 1 to Anthropic the same mechanism (built too much, monetizing surplus to buy time) or a structurally different one (intentional infrastructure-as-a-service business model alongside a separate Colossus 2 buildout)? **The analog partially applies because**, while xAI does have Colossus 2 as a credible primary buildout, the speed at which Colossus 1 was offered to a direct rival within twelve months of construction suggests the original primary-use-case demand for Grok was overestimated — exactly the Williams pattern, but not yet at the bankruptcy-trajectory severity.
