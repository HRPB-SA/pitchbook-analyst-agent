### §11 CFA PREP
**Level I · December 2026**
Domain: Equity — IPO Valuation (continuing from June 4 rotation)
Concept: Sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) valuation for multi-segment companies vs. blended revenue multiple.
**Vignette:** SpaceX IPO prices at \$135/share, \$1.77T implied valuation. 2025 consolidated revenue: \$18.67B. Starlink connectivity segment: \$1.19B profit last quarter (\~\$4.76B annualized, est.). xAI segment: burning \~\$10B in 2026. Falcon launch services: government contract-dependent, EBITDA-positive.
**Question A:** An analyst applies a blended 95x EV/Revenue to SpaceX's consolidated \$18.67B revenue base and arrives at a \$1.77T valuation. A second analyst uses SOTP, applying a 25x multiple to Starlink (telecom/internet infra comp), a 15x multiple to Falcon (defense contractor comp), and a 50x optionality multiple to Starship/xAI. Under what conditions do these two methods produce the same result, and which is more defensible for a CFA candidate to use in a written justification?
**Answer:** They converge only if the blended multiple happens to equal the weighted average of the SOTP segment multiples — a coincidence, not a methodology. For a CFA candidate, SOTP is more defensible because it: (1) respects segment-level comparables rather than forcing a single multiple onto structurally different cash flow profiles; (2) isolates the optionality value of Starship/xAI rather than embedding it in a consolidated revenue multiple; (3) makes assumptions explicit and independently testable. The blended 95x method is not wrong — it is the method implied by the IPO price — but it conflates Starlink's quality (profitable, subscription, low churn) with xAI's profile (burning \$10B, no standalone revenue disclosure). Wrong-answer trap: concluding that the blended method is "simpler therefore acceptable" — simplicity is not a defense in valuation when segment profiles diverge materially.
Harrison connection: This is the AIBQ CE problem restated as a CFA question. The xAI/SpaceX CE score is undefined precisely because the revenue base is consolidated and the loss-generating segment has no standalone financial disclosure. The same methodological ambiguity that produces an undefined AIBQ CE score produces a disputable IPO valuation — they are the same problem from different angles.

That constraint is independent of company quality.
**Where it diverges:** In 2000, the companies going public were often pre-revenue. In 2026, the risk is multiple compression on real businesses, not business failure. The Morningstar \$780B SpaceX fair value is the 2026 equivalent of what sober analysts said about Amazon at \$400 in 1999 — right company, wrong price.
**Implication:** The allocation physics problem is the dominant IPO risk for the second half of 2026. If SPCX absorbs \$75B of institutional capital in June, what remains for Anthropic in October? Databricks in Q4? The sequencing of the IPO queue — not the quality of the companies — may determine which ones achieve their target valuations.
