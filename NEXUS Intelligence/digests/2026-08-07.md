The borrower retains ownership of the securities but pledges them against the loan principal; if the value of the collateral falls below the lender's required threshold (the margin call level), the borrower must either post additional collateral or the lender may liquidate the pledged securities to recover the loan.<br><br>\*\*Mechanism (the why):\*\* The critical feature of a margin loan against illiquid private equity (as opposed to publicly traded securities) is that "liquidation" means finding a secondary buyer in a thin market — there is no exchange. The lender's ability to enforce a margin call is therefore constrained by the available secondary market depth, which in private AI stakes is narrow and price-sensitive. This means: (a) lenders charge higher rates to compensate for illiquidity risk; (b) LTV ratios are set conservatively (typically 20–40% for private stakes vs. 50–70% for liquid equity); and (c) the forced-sale risk is real but slow — it takes weeks to months, not hours, compared to a public margin call. The \$10B SoftBank loan implies that lenders believe OpenAI's equity has sufficient depth in the secondary market to enforce the covenant if needed.<br><br>\*\*Live a
nchor:\*\* The SoftBank / OpenAI \$10B margin loan (§4) is the exact structure described above. The analytical tool: to evaluate this event, estimate the LTV ratio. If SoftBank's OpenAI stake is worth \~\$X billion at the \$852B company valuation and the loan is \$10B, then LTV = \$10B / \$X. If LTV exceeds 40%, the position is leveraged beyond conservative private-equity margin-loan norms, and the forced-sale risk is materially elevated.
