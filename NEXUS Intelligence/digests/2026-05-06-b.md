# 🎓 13. CFA PREP
**Domain.** Ethics — Standard I(B) Independence and Objectivity. (I(A) completed May 5.)
**Concept.** I(B) requires reasonable care and judgment to maintain independence and objectivity, and to avoid offering, soliciting, or accepting any gift, benefit, compensation, or consideration that could reasonably be expected to compromise objectivity. The most common exam trap is the modest-gift rule. The key distinguishing variable is *recurrence*: a one-time modest gift may be acceptable with disclosure, but recurring or escalating gifts must be refused or escalated to compliance regardless of value.
**Practice question (vignette).** Sara Chen, CFA, senior equity analyst at a sell-side bank. A covered company hosts her on a two-day site visit: flights, two nights at a four-star hotel, group dinners at a Michelin-starred restaurant on both evenings, private factory tour. Total estimated value \~\$3,200. The same issuer made a similar invitation last year, which Chen also accepted. Both visits disclosed and approved by her supervisor in writing. Chen has maintained a Buy rating on the issuer for eighteen months. Which best describes Chen's situation under Standard I(B)?
A. Chen has not violated I(B) because hospitality was disclosed and approved by compliance in writing.
B. Chen has violated I(B) because the value exceeds modest-gift thresholds and recurrence creates a presumption of compromised objectivity that disclosure alone does not cure.
C. Chen has violated I(B) only if her Buy rating was inconsistent with fundamentals during the eighteen-month period.
D. Chen has not violated I(B) because reasonable due diligence requires accepting issuer-paid travel when independent travel is not feasible.
**Answer.** **B.**
I(B) is concerned with *reasonable expectation* of compromised objectivity, not actual compromise. Combination of value above modest-gift thresholds, recurrence (second invitation), and Buy rating maintained throughout creates a textbook reasonable-expectation problem. Disclosure does not cure this — disclosure plus approval is procedurally sufficient only when the gift itself is within modest parameters.
A fails: confuses *procedural compliance* with *substantive compliance*. Both required.
C fails: the *outcomes* trap. Standard does not require evidence the rating was wrong — only that a reasonable observer would expect objectivity compromised.
D fails: the *necessity* defense. Issuer-paid travel not justified by impracticality; firm should bear the cost.
**Harrison connection.** Maps to the same discipline that keeps the AIBQ framework defensible — substance over form, disclosure as floor not ceiling.

# 📜 19. THE ANALOG
The closest historical analog to today's E11 is **Apple's 1997 acceptance of a \$150 million equity investment from Microsoft, paired with a five-year cross-licensing patent agreement and a commitment by Microsoft to ship Internet Explorer and Microsoft Office for Macintosh.** At the time, Apple was nine months from insolvency, Microsoft was the dominant operating system platform and Apple's most strategically threatening rival, and Steve Jobs had just returned to the company. The deal was announced at MacWorld Boston by Jobs to a famously hostile audience. The mechanism: Microsoft accepted that an alternative-platform Apple was strategically valuable as antitrust insulation, Apple accepted that survival required tactical alignment with the platform rival, and both companies extracted asymmetric value from a deal that surprised the market. Apple's stock went from \$3.30 split-adjusted in 1997 to multiple decades of compounding leadership. Microsoft's antitrust exposure was reduced. Neither company suffered strategic damage from the alignment despite the apparent contradiction. The mechanism in today's Anthropic/SpaceX deal is structurally similar: a dominant compute provider with surplus capacity provides infrastructure to a strategic rival, both extract asymmetric value, and the public hostility is reframed as commercial pragmatism. The one question: does the Anthropic/SpaceX relationship deepen over time toward genuine strategic alignment (orbital compute formalization, joint development), or does it remain a discrete capacity transaction? **Verdict: the analog applies — rival-to-rival infrastructure deals tend to expand, not contract, when the underlying capacity utilization economics work for both parties.**
