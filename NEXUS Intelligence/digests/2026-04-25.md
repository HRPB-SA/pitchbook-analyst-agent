## 🎓 13. CFA PREP
**Domain:** Ethics — Standard I(C) Misrepresentation (Session 3 of 28)
**Concept:** Members must not make any misrepresentations relating to investment analysis, recommendations, actions, or other professional activities. Misrepresentation includes false statements, omissions that cause a statement to be misleading, and plagiarism. The most common exam trap: an *omission* of material information can be a misrepresentation even when every affirmative statement made is technically accurate. The Standard applies to both oral and written communications.
**Practice Question:** A PitchBook analyst covering Anthropic publishes a valuation note stating: “Anthropic achieved \$30B in ARR as of April 2026, confirmed by Bloomberg.” The note omits that this figure is gross revenue before a \$5.4B revenue-share payment to Amazon and Google. A reader who relies on this note would materially overestimate Anthropic’s net retained revenue. Which of the following best characterizes the analyst’s conduct under Standard I(C)?
A) No violation — the \$30B ARR figure is factually accurate and Bloomberg-sourced.
B) Violation — omitting the revenue-share obligation creates a misleading impression of financial performance.
C) Violation — the analyst should have used net ARR rather than gross ARR regardless of what was sourced.
D) No violation — Standard I(C) applies only to recommendations, not to factual disclosures.
**Answer: B.** The factual accuracy of the gross figure does not cure the misleading omission. Standard I(C) is violated when accurate statements, taken together, create a false impression. **Why A fails:** Accuracy of the stated fact is not a defense when the omitted context is material. **Why C fails:** The Standard does not prescribe which revenue metric to use — it requires that whatever is stated not create a misleading impression. Gross ARR with disclosure of the revenue-share structure would comply. **Why D fails:** Standard I(C) expressly covers “investment analysis” and “other professional activities” — it is not limited to recommendations.
**Harrison connection:** The gross-vs-net ARR distinction in your \$100B Entry Fee model is exactly this issue — the ASC 606 rows you added to the Anthropic model address this risk directly.

## 📁 19. THE ANALOG
**The analog:** NTT DoCoMo’s i-mode platform in Japan, 1999–2004. DoCoMo became the mandatory distribution layer for internet-connected mobile services in Japan by providing the compliance and billing infrastructure that foreign internet companies couldn’t build themselves. Foreign content providers paid 9% of revenue to DoCoMo for access to the distribution network. NEC was a hardware partner. The model worked until smartphone OS platforms (iOS/Android) disintermediated the carrier layer entirely.
**The question that determines whether the mechanism holds:** Is Anthropic the content provider (paying NEC for distribution), or is NEC the content provider (paying Anthropic for model access)? The economic direction determines whether this is DoCoMo-style distribution tax or a genuine co-sell partnership.
**Verdict:** The analog partially applies. NEC provides trust infrastructure the way DoCoMo provided billing infrastructure. The disintermediation risk is different: not from a new OS platform, but from the Japanese government building a domestic sovereign AI stack that sidelines both NEC and Anthropic simultaneously.
