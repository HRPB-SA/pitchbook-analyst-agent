# Global Unicorn Tracker - Q2 2026

Late-Stage Company Research. As of June 30, 2026 (Q2 close). Primary basis:
PitchBook deal-level unicorn tracker (1,743 active / $8,232.5B aggregate). Two
other bases are used only where noted and never blended: the Morningstar
PitchBook Unicorn 20 daily model marks (the 20 marquee names), and the Unicorn
Monitor (used for the three-mark secondary validation and the vertical index
returns). Every figure below is labeled to its basis.

## Introduction

The private unicorn universe closed Q2 2026 at an aggregate post-money valuation
of $8,232.5 billion across 1,743 companies, up roughly 13% year over year from
$7,305.4 billion. The number is best read as an accounting sum rather than a
market value: it adds each company's most recent post-money mark, a six-month-old
mark and a four-year-old mark carrying identical weight, with no venue in which
$8.2 trillion of private stock could actually clear. Understanding how little of
that total is fresh, and how few names carry it, is most of the analysis.

Two facts define the quarter. First, value creation has re-concentrated at the
very top to a degree not seen since the dataset began: two companies now hold 22%
of the entire universe, and the top ten hold far more. Second, one theme accounts
for the growth outright. Artificial intelligence added $1.59 trillion of
aggregate value in six months while the whole universe grew $0.93 trillion,
which means non-AI verticals contracted by roughly $660 billion in aggregate. The
market did not broaden in Q2; it narrowed onto a handful of AI franchises, and
everything else in this report is a consequence of that.

## Part I - Market overview: the leaders

### The quarter that belonged to five companies

Q2's capital concentrated into a handful of megarounds. The five largest deals of
the quarter totaled roughly $87.1 billion, and a single company, Anthropic,
raised $65 billion of it at a $965 billion post-money, or 75% of the top five and
5.4 times the second-largest deal (Prometheus, $12 billion to a $41 billion
post). The remainder of the top five were Anduril ($5 billion to $61 billion),
ByteDance ($3 billion to $370 billion), and Isomorphic Labs ($2.1 billion). Four
of the five are AI companies. Anthropic alone was 16.4% of all first-half deal
value ($65 billion of $396.1 billion).

What makes the "five companies" framing literal rather than rhetorical is the
cliff below the top five. Deal size falls under $2 billion by the sixth name and
under $1 billion almost immediately after; the top ten reached 68.9% of sized
deal value and the top twenty-five 78.5%. The quarter's headline is therefore not
a description of the market but of five cap tables, four of them in AI. For a
research reader the implication is that any statement about "unicorn fundraising
in Q2" is, to first order, a statement about Anthropic.

### The top of the market: fresh prices, stale prices, and secondary validation

The marquee valuations raise the question of whether they are current marks or old
ones carried forward, and whether anyone is actually trading at them. For the
twenty most closely tracked names the Unicorn 20 file supplies a daily model mark,
the Monitor supplies the last-round post-money, and the Monitor secondary tape
supplies an implied value from actual secondary trades. In aggregate the model
marks sit 15.2% above the last-round marks for the eighteen names with a round
(model $2,935.7 billion versus round $2,549.6 billion), which is what a rising
market should produce.

The secondary tape is the arbiter, and it disagrees most where the dollars are
largest. Secondary-implied value sits below the model for twelve of the twenty,
and far below for the two biggest at the 6/30 close: SpaceX trades 32% under its
$2,026 billion model mark (a roughly $650 billion gap) and Anthropic 22% under its
$1,272 billion mark (roughly $282 billion), with OpenAI 9% and Databricks 7% below.
Those two model marks caught SpaceX just after its June IPO and Anthropic at a
fresh round, running ahead of a secondary market that had not repriced. Below the
megacaps the sign flips into genuine up-round pressure: secondaries trade above the
last round for Applied Intuition (+36%), Anysphere (+30%), Anduril (+22%), and
Revolut (+16%), meaning their stale marks understate them. At the bottom, all three
prices have already repriced down together (Ripple, Epic Games, Kraken). The
durable point is that a private mark is only as reliable as the agreement among its
three prices, and in Q2 that agreement is thinnest exactly at the top of the book.

### The mean-median gap: how concentration actually shows up

The mechanics of concentration are visible in two paired statistics. The median
unicorn post-money in 2026 is $2.0 billion while the average is $18.19 billion, a
9.1x mean-to-median ratio, up from 3.1x in 2025 and 2.8x in 2024. The same shape
appears in round sizes: the median deal is $200 million against a $1,143 million
average, a 5.7x skew and the widest in the eleven-year series. What both ratios
say is that the aggregate is being pulled upward by a handful of enormous marks
while the typical company barely moved. The $8.2 trillion headline rose because a
dozen names re-rated, not because the median unicorn appreciated; a reader who
treats the aggregate as a market-wide signal will systematically overstate the
experience of the median company. The two distributions should be shown side by
side, because the gap between them is the story.

### Step-ups: the active market is functioning

For the companies that did raise, the terms were healthy. The median valuation
step-up (new round post-money over prior) reached 2.20x in 2026, up from 2.05x in
2025 and the highest since 2021's 2.64x peak; the median time between rounds fell
to 0.98 years from 1.21 in 2025 and 1.45 in 2024, so rounds are clearing faster;
and down rounds have nearly vanished, with six in the first half worth $3.85
billion against 31 in 2025 and 34 in 2024. The market is clearing up, not
stalling or repricing down. One nuance keeps this from being a broad "all-clear":
the step-up leaders are small names, not giants. Hark stepped up 151x, Blitzy and
SendCutSend 30x each, all sub-$7 billion companies, while the giants stepped up in
dollars but not in multiple. Velocity lives at the bottom of the size
distribution; value lives at the top. And the metric only describes companies that
raised, which, as the dormancy analysis shows, is well under half the universe.

### AI: two-fifths of count, three-fifths of value

AI's share of the universe has crossed a threshold. On the primary tracker's
AI-aggregate basis (each company counted once), AI is now 702 of 1,743 unicorns
(40.3%) and $5,076 billion of $8,232.5 billion (61.7%). The value share running
twenty points ahead of the count share means AI companies are individually far
more valuable than the universe average. The trajectory is steep: AI aggregate
value grew from $3,487 billion at end-2025 to $5,076 billion, up 45.6% in six
months, and AI accounted for 109 of the 178 first-half mints (61%). The
concentration is more extreme in flows than in stock: AI is a larger share of new
minting and new deal value than of the standing universe, so its dominance is
still increasing. (Two narrower AI cuts exist and should not be confused with this
one: the AI vertical tag, 865 companies on a nonexclusive basis, and the Monitor
curated AI index, 430 companies at $4,143.7 billion. This section uses the
702-company AI-aggregate series throughout.)

## Part II - Verticals

### SaaS and AI dominance, and the steep drop-off after

Ranked by tagged value (primary tracker, nonexclusive tags), the market is two
themes and a tail: SaaS at $6.44 trillion (968 companies), AI at $6.02 trillion
(865), FinTech a distant third at $2.02 trillion (436), then a cliff to Space
Technology $1.87 trillion (34), Mobility $0.83 trillion, CloudTech $0.74 trillion,
and HealthTech $0.45 trillion. The top two verticals alone carry about $12.5
trillion of tagged value; from the fourth vertical down, each is a rounding
difference against AI and SaaS. Because tags are nonexclusive, vertical counts sum
to well over the 1,743 companies and vertical values sum to about 1.5x the
universe, so they must never be added to a total; a company building AI-powered
SaaS is counted in both leading verticals, and the apparent breadth of the map is
largely an artifact of multi-tagging.

Value per company exposes where concentration is total. Space Technology carries
$1.87 trillion across 34 companies, about $55 billion each, but that average is
almost entirely SpaceX; strip it and the vertical is ordinary. It is the cleanest
example of a vertical aggregate that is really one cap table wearing a sector
label.

### The narrow investable universe

Of 1,743 unicorns, very few are actually accessible at size with a fresh mark and
secondary liquidity. The value is top-heavy (the top ten names carry a large share
of the $8.2 trillion while the median company is $2.0 billion), and the investable
set collapses once real filters are applied. Layering the three that matter, a
recent mark (under twelve months), meaningful size (say $5 billion or more), and
some secondary liquidity, takes the list from 1,743 to a few dozen names. The
practical consequence is that "diversifying across the unicorn universe" is mostly
unavailable: the accessible book is a concentrated set of large, AI-heavy,
US-domiciled companies, and a vertical-blind portfolio drawn from it would be a
SaaS-and-AI portfolio by default.

### Count and valuation versus performance: the vertical return layer

Size hides performance, and the two do not line up. On 2026 medians, AI ran an RVVC
of 1.84x and a step-up of 2.35x across 249 deals, and SaaS 1.42x and 2.18x across
176, healthy but not the fastest. The fastest appreciators are smaller verticals:
Robotics and Drones posted the highest median RVVC at 2.03x (average 5.66x),
CleanTech ran hot at 3.30x RVVC and 2.77x step-up, while Crypto and Blockchain
cooled sharply, its median RVVC falling from 2.62x to 1.11x. The read is that the
biggest verticals are not the fastest-appreciating ones: on a value-versus-velocity
grid, SaaS is big but slow, Robotics and CleanTech are small but fast, and the
laggards (Cybersecurity RVVC 0.66x, E-Commerce 0.38x) are stepping up in headline
price while creating little value per dollar invested.

The mark-to-market layer confirms the same hierarchy over three years. On the
Monitor vertical indexes (6/16/2023 to 6/30/2026, annualized), AI returned 58.1%
per year (a cumulative +302.6%), Enterprise SaaS 34.2%, and Cybersecurity 22.2%,
the only three verticals to beat the Global TME public benchmark of 19.6%
annualized (+72.4% cumulative); AI compounded at roughly three times the public
rate. FinTech, the third-largest vertical by value, returned just 12.9% and sits
below the benchmark, large in stock but weak in flow. One caution: these index
returns are computed on infrequently updated private marks, which mechanically
suppresses measured volatility, so any risk-adjusted comparison built on them will
overstate the asset class.

### AI as growth engine and concentration risk

AI is simultaneously the market's entire growth case and its single largest point
of failure. It drove +$1.59 trillion of a +$0.93 trillion universe move, meaning
the rest of the market shrank in aggregate around it; it is now 62% of value, four
of the top five rounds, and 61% of new mints. Because AI marks move together and
carry so much of the index, a re-rate in a few frontier names moves the whole
aggregate. The scenario is quantifiable: a 25% markdown of AI marks would remove
about $1.27 trillion from the aggregate, roughly 15% of the entire universe, from
one theme repricing. Underweighting AI is a bet against nearly two-thirds of all
private tech value; overweighting it concentrates a portfolio in a handful of cap
tables that the secondary tape already marks below their models. That
inseparability of engine and risk is the defining tension of the quarter.

### The 2021 vintage and the forward read

The 2021 boom cohort is the best available guide to how today's mega-cohort will
age. Of 635 unicorns minted in 2021, five years on 470 remain active (74%), 102
have exited (16%), and the rest have impaired: 34 fallen below $1 billion while
still private, 15 fallen at exit (a down-IPO or down-M&A below unicorn status,
which is a distinct and underreported failure mode), and 13 bankrupt, 62 impaired
in all. The headline is that three-quarters of the cohort remain illiquid four and
a half years on, and that even the exits include fifteen companies that crossed
the line below their unicorn mark. With a median time to exit of 8.47 years, the
2021 class will not fully clear until roughly 2029, and the ten-year 2021 funds
mature between 2028 and 2031, a forcing function that will resolve the untested
boom-era marks through markdown, M&A, or wind-down. Applying that survival curve
to today's 702 AI unicorns is the forward scenario: if the AI class of 2026 ages
like the SaaS class of 2021, a quarter impairs and three-quarters sit illiquid
into the next decade, with the added risk that AI marks are more correlated and
more concentrated than 2021's were.

## Part III - Deal dynamics, capital, and exits

### Who is funding it, and how the syndicates are shaped

Round structure has herded into large syndicates even as round counts fell.
Six-or-more-investor rounds are 60.9% of 2026 known-investor rounds, near the
2021 peak of 64%, so the fewer deals that happen are crowded. Yet participation
breadth is not recovering with the dollars: quarterly investor participation
peaked at 2,892 in Q3 2021, troughed at 895 in Q4 2023, rose to 1,415 in Q1 2026,
then fell back to 995 in Q2, even as Q2 deal value stayed high, which is the
signature of fewer, larger, more concentrated rounds. The league table is led by
Sequoia Capital (491 unicorn deals since 2016), Tiger Global (472), Andreessen
Horowitz (454), Accel (382), and Coatue (344); the top of the investor list, like
the top of the company list, is a short one.

### Exits and the reopening of the IPO window

Exit value in 2026 reached $2,110.9 billion across 50 exits, the strongest year
in the dataset, but it is one event. SpaceX's IPO alone was $1,690 billion, 80% of
all 2026 exit value; strip it and the ex-SpaceX figure is $420.7 billion, which is
still the best exit year since 2021 but a fraction of the headline. Both numbers
should always be quoted together. Public listings drove 85% of exit value and the
IPO window has clearly reopened after three shut years, with SpaceX, Cerebras ($34
billion), and Quantinuum ($14 billion) all listing in the quarter. The counterweight
is duration: the median time to exit rose to 8.47 years (a 9.17-year peak in 2025),
so the companies clearing now are old vintages working through a backlog, not fresh
2023-2024 names. The exit market is open, but it is draining a reservoir, not
running current.

## Part IV - PitchBook Business Quality (PBQ) and valuations

### Why the valuation table is not a quality ranking

The naive ranking is the price ladder: Anthropic, OpenAI, ByteDance, Stripe,
Databricks, Waymo, and down. It orders companies by expense, not by quality, and
price is a function of capital raised as much as of value created; two names at the
same valuation can differ by an order of magnitude in capital efficiency. It is
also partly an artifact of staleness, since two of the top ten ride marks more than
a year old that a fresh round could move in either direction. A quality-adjusted
ranking would break the rank order of the price ladder, which is precisely why the
PBQ column has to sit beside price rather than beneath it.

### Expanding PBQ, and using secondaries as the estimate-integrity input

A full PBQ or AIBQ composite requires operating inputs (revenue quality, margin,
customer concentration, compute independence, governance, moat) that none of the
three files contain, so the score cannot be extended to all twenty from this data;
composites exist for only six frontier names from the prior edition (Databricks
8.7, Anthropic 7.7, SpaceX 7.5, OpenAI 4.2, xAI 3.8, SSI 2.3), and their spread is
itself the argument for the framework, since OpenAI, the third most valuable
company in the world, scores below the midpoint. What the current data does add is
one measurable dimension of quality: estimate integrity, the agreement among a
name's three marks. Companies whose model, round, and secondary prices cluster
tightly (Canva, Stripe, Rippling) have corroborated, reliable valuations; those
whose prices diverge widely (Neuralink at +441% model over round, Ripple, Kraken)
have low mark reliability, which is itself a governance and valuation-quality
signal. Flagging which of the twenty have real secondary liquidity versus which are
marks only is the practical first step; the full scorecard and a PBQ-per-dollar
ranking (quality bought per dollar of valuation, which would invert the price
ladder) both await the analyst-generated composites and should be scoped as
pending, not derived from this data.

## Part V - Valuations and fallen unicorns

### Where the value sits

The distribution is power-law, not normal. The top two companies, Anthropic ($965
billion) and OpenAI ($852 billion), hold $1.82 trillion, 22% of the entire $8.2
trillion universe, and the top ten hold a majority of it, while the median company
is $2.0 billion. Concentration this extreme means the index and the median have
decoupled: the aggregate can rise on a dozen marks while the typical unicorn does
nothing. A cumulative-share (Lorenz) curve is the right exhibit, and the one-number
summary is that a handful of names carry a share of value that a normal
distribution could never produce.

### Latest valuation and the coverage gap

Freshness is good at the very top and decays fast below it. Anthropic ($965
billion) and ByteDance ($370 billion) carry May 2026 marks and OpenAI ($852
billion) a March 2026 mark, but below the leaders the marks age quickly, with names
in the top ten carrying 2023 marks (a mid-2023 FinTech mark, a May 2023 SHEIN
mark). The hard measure, computed from actual latest-round dates across the active
book, is that 47.6% of unicorns have not raised in more than 24 months and 37.1%
in more than 36 months. That is the credibility test for the $8.2 trillion
headline: nearly half of it rests on marks that have not been tested in a financing
for two years or more. The universe is two-tier, a fresh-marked frontier of a few
dozen names and a large stale-marked middle whose repricing risk is invisible in
the aggregate, and the aggregate should be split into fresh-marked and
stale-marked dollars rather than quoted whole. (This 47.6% figure, from the primary
tracker's latest-round dates, is authoritative and supersedes an earlier
qualifying-date proxy of 78.5% that overstated staleness.)

### Step-ups and RVVC: velocity is back

Read together, the two velocity metrics say the active market is creating real
value again. Median RVVC recovered to 1.42x in 2026, up from 0.93x in 2025 and a
0.23x trough in 2023, a full recovery above the roughly 0.73x pre-boom baseline,
while the median step-up of 2.20x and time-between-rounds of 0.98 years confirm
rounds are clearing up and fast. RVVC above 1.0 means each raised dollar created
more than a dollar of new valuation, the opposite of the 2023 dilution regime. The
one caveat is the same skew that runs through the whole report: average RVVC is
2.73x against the 1.42x median, so velocity, like value, is concentrated in a few
names, and the metric describes only companies that raised, not the dormant
majority.

### Fallen unicorns

There are 245 fallen unicorns to date, but the flow of new falls has collapsed even
as the accumulated stock is large: just six down rounds in the first half of 2026
worth $3.85 billion, against annual peaks of 34 falls in 2024. When companies do
fall, they fall hard, the median down-round post-money is $524.5 million and the
median markdown is 67% below the prior peak, so the typical fallen unicorn lands at
roughly a third of its high-water mark, just under the $1 billion line. The 2021
cohort supplies the cleanest breakdown and the most underreported finding: of its
failures, 34 fell while still private, 13 went bankrupt, and 15 fell at exit,
crossing out below unicorn status through a down-IPO or down-M&A. That last
category is a failure mode the headline exit count hides, and separating fallen and
still-private from fallen-at-exit is the honest way to present attrition. The low
current flow of falls should not be read as health, because the 47.6% of the book
carrying untested marks is the shadow inventory from which the next wave will come.

## The through-line

The $8.2 trillion headline is real, concentrated, and mostly untested. It is
concentrated at the top (two names hold 22%, the top five took 62% of Q2 deal
flow), fresh only at that top (the marquee marks, corroborated by secondaries to
within roughly a tenth) and stale across the middle (47.6% of the book untested for
two years or more), and it is, more than anything, an AI story: AI added more value
in six months than the entire universe did, and it is now three-fifths of all
private tech value on two-fifths of the names. The active market is functioning
again (step-ups 2.2x, RVVC 1.42x, down rounds near zero), the IPO window has
reopened (though 80% of exit value is one SpaceX listing), and the falls have
slowed, but the 2021 vintage shows what waits on the other side of a boom: a
quarter impaired, three-quarters still illiquid five years on, and a fund clock
that forces resolution between 2028 and 2031. The number to watch is not the
aggregate, which will keep climbing on a dozen AI marks, but the spread between the
tested few and the untested many, and whether AI's marks hold when the secondary
tape already says they are rich.
